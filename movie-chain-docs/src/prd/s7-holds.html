7The Seat-Hold Engine

Everything else in this system is ordinary CRUD. This is the part that is genuinely hard, the part that will produce your worst production incidents, and the part worth designing carefully before writing a line of code.

7.1 What the engine must guarantee

IDGuaranteeWhat it means precisely
G-1ExclusivityAt any instant, a given seat at a given screening is held or owned by at most one party. Not “usually”; not “unless two requests arrive in the same millisecond”.
G-2Bounded holdsA hold expires automatically after its window with no user action, no browser still open, and no scheduled job on the critical path.
G-3No lost inventoryA seat never remains blocked after its hold has expired, even if the application crashed mid-request.
G-4No double saleTwo confirmed bookings for the same seat at the same screening must be impossible, regardless of cache state or provider behaviour.
G-5Tenant containmentA hold is visible and contestable only within its own tenant. Nyati's holds are invisible to Prestige, including through timing or error messages.
G-7No account requiredA hold can be acquired, kept and converted to a paid booking by someone with no account at all (FR-70). Ownership of a hold is therefore not an identity question.
G-6Honest moneyA customer is never charged for a seat they do not receive. If that ever occurs, it is detected and refunded automatically without a support ticket.

7.2 The race condition, stated plainly

The obvious implementation is wrong, and it is worth seeing exactly why before seeing the fix.

Reading the seat's status and then writing it are two separate round trips. Between them, any number of other requests can run. On a quiet Tuesday this never manifests. At 18:00 on the Friday a blockbuster opens — precisely when hundreds of people are competing for the same few good seats — it manifests constantly.

The fix is never to check more carefully. It is to collapse the check and the act into a single operation the datastore guarantees is indivisible.

7.3 The design

We use two datastores, each for what it is genuinely good at.

Redis — the fast path

Holds the contention. SET key value NX EX 300Redis — SET command is atomic and gives us expiry for free — no timer, no cron job, no code that can fail to run.

It also absorbs seat-map polling, which would otherwise be the heaviest read load in the system.

PostgreSQL — the truth

Holds the record. PRIMARY KEY (screening_id, seat_id) makes a double sale structurally impossible, whatever Redis does.

Survives restarts, is backed up, and is what the business reconciles against.

The rule that keeps this sound

Correctness must never depend on the cache. Redis can be flushed, can fail over, and can lose a recent write. So the durable uniqueness constraint in PostgreSQL is not redundancy — it is the actual guarantee. Redis makes the common case fast and self-expiring; Postgres makes the guarantee true.

If you must choose only one, choose Postgres and accept a sweeper job. Never choose only Redis.

Who owns a hold, when there is no user

Guarantee G-7 says the buyer may have no account. A hold therefore cannot belong to a user id — it belongs to a signed hold token the server issues and the client returns.

The hold token
hold_token = sign({ hold_id, screening_id, tenant_id, exp }, server_key)

  · opaque and signed — the client cannot alter or forge one
  · returned by POST /holds, replayed on POST /bookings
  · possession IS the claim to the seat; no identity is consulted
  · lifetime exactly matches the hold, so a stale token is self-evidently dead

Why not a session cookie or a user id?
  · a guest has neither (FR-70)
  · a signed token works identically for guests, logged-in consumers
    and box-office clerks — one mechanism, not three
  · it survives a page refresh, which a memory-held id does not

Note that tenant_id is inside the signed token. It is placed there by the server from screening.tenant_id — never supplied by the caller — so the booking step does not have to re-derive it, and a token minted for a Nyati screening can never be presented against a Prestige one.

Key design

Redis key structure
lock:t:{tenant_id}:scr:{screening_id}:seat:{seat_id}  ->  {hold_id}   TTL 300s

Why the tenant is in the key, first:
  · G-5 — a scan or key-space notification cannot span tenants
  · a mis-scoped screening_id cannot collide across tenants
  · per-tenant memory usage becomes measurable for cost attribution
  · a tenant can be evicted from cache wholesale when offboarded

Acquiring a multi-seat hold

A customer selecting three seatsRedis — Distributed locks must get all three or none — a partial hold is a poor experience and leaks inventory. The operation must therefore be atomic across several keys, which means a Lua script (Redis executes scripts atomically) rather than a sequence of commands.

Lua — all-or-nothing hold
-- KEYS: one per seat.  ARGV[1] = hold_id, ARGV[2] = ttl seconds
for i = 1, #KEYS do
  if redis.call('EXISTS', KEYS[i]) == 1 then
    return {0, i}                       -- report which seat lost
  end
end
for i = 1, #KEYS do
  redis.call('SET', KEYS[i], ARGV[1], 'NX', 'EX', ARGV[2])
end
return {1, 0}

Because the whole script runs atomically, no other request can interleave between the existence checks and the writes. This is the single-operation principle applied to a set of seats rather than one.

The row in bold deserves your attention

“Payment succeeded but the seats are gone” is the failure that generates furious customers and manual refunds. It is rare, it is unavoidable in principle, and it must be handled automatically.

The mitigation has three parts. First, refuse to initiate payment when less than 45 seconds of hold remain — offer an extension instead. Second, re-verify hold ownership immediately before capture. Third, if capture nonetheless succeeds against a lost hold, refund automatically within seconds and tell the customer what happened. A customer who is refunded before they notice the problem does not become a support ticket.

7.5 The sweeper

Redis expiry silently removes the key. The durable seat_lock row remains, still marked held, so a reconciling job is required.

Sweeper query — runs every 30 seconds, leader-elected
-- deliberately NOT tenant-scoped: this is a platform maintenance job
-- running with elevated privileges, outside the RLS path. It is one of
-- only two such jobs in the system, and both are reviewed as security code.
DELETE FROM seat_lock
 WHERE status = 'held'
   AND expires_at < now() - interval '30 seconds'
 RETURNING tenant_id, screening_id, seat_id;

The thirty-second grace period matters: it ensures the durable row outlives the Redis key, so the sweeper never deletes a row whose hold is in the middle of being converted to a sale.

Why the sweeper is not the mechanism

It would be possible to skip Redis and rely on the sweeper alone. The reason not to is latency of release. With a 30-second sweeper, a seat can sit unsellable for up to 30 seconds after its hold dies. On a sold-out blockbuster on-sale, those seconds are lost revenue and visible customer frustration — the seat map shows a seat as taken when it is actually free.

Redis TTL releases the seat the instant it expires. The sweeper exists only to keep the durable record honest, which is a background concern rather than a customer-facing one.

7.6 Seat states

Note the deliberate asymmetry in what customers see. Internally a seat may be AVAILABLE, HELD by this customer, HELD by another, SOLD or REDEEMED. Externally the seat map shows only three states: free, taken, and yours. Distinguishing “held by someone else” from “sold” in the UI would leak commercial information and invite abuse — a competitor could infer demand in real time by watching which seats are merely being considered.

7.7 Tuning the window

Five minutes is the default, configurable per tenant between two and fifteen. The trade-off is direct and should be revisited with real data after launch.

WindowEffect on completionEffect on inventory
2 minutesToo short for M-Pesa, which requires the customer to confirm on their handset. Abandonment rises sharply.Inventory turns over quickly
5 minutes defaultComfortable for card and M-Pesa, including a mistyped number and one retry.Acceptable even on a sell-out
10+ minutesLittle additional completion; most customers who will pay have paid within four.On a sold-out screening, abandoned holds visibly starve real buyers

The measurement that should drive any change is the distribution of time from hold to payment among customers who complete. Set the window at roughly the 95th percentile of that distribution, and re-check it whenever a payment method is added.
